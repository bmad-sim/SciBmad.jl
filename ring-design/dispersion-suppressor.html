2. Dispersion Suppressor¶

Introduction¶

To connect an arc to the following straight section in a sextant, the dispersion in the straight section must be suppressed to zero while minimally changing the beta functions. For a 90° lattice, this can be approximately achieved by using two arc FODO cells with the bends at half strength. For FODO cells with a different phase advance, dispersion suppressors can also be constructed from the last two cells by changing their bend strengths. For 60° FODO cells, for example, the first cell has zero bend strength, and the last has the full bend strength. To get the dispersion and its derivative at the end of the dispersion suppressor exactly zero, the last two quadrupoles can be minimally varied.

Example: Forward Dispersion Suppressor¶

The procedure for creating the forward dispersion suppressor is outlined below. The reverse dispersion suppressor is left as an exercise.

  1. Start with the lattice of chapter 1, which contains the arc cells with 90° phase advance. If you did not do the exercises of chapter 1, include the lattice file of that chapter:

include("lattices/01-fodo.jl");

  1. Define a new half-strength bend bh, and the two special quadrupoles that will be varied to make the dispersion \(\eta_x\) and its derivative \(\eta_x'\) zero. Because these special quadrupoles are in the forward sextants (F), and are the first special quadrupoles (1), they are named qff1 and qdf1:

@elements begin
  bh = SBend(L=Lc*(θ/2)/(2*sin(θ/4)), angle=θ/2, e1=θ/4, e2=θ/4) # Half-strength bend
  qff1 = Quadrupole(L=0.5, Kn1=0.3)
  qdf1 = Quadrupole(L=0.5, Kn1=-0.3)
end;

  1. Define the line of the figure above, with the half-strength bends and the special quadrupoles, and a Beamline with it:

dispsupf = [qf, d1, bh, d2, qd, d1, bh, d2, qff1, d1, bh, d2, qdf1, d1, bh, d2]
ds_f = Beamline(dispsupf, species_ref=electron, pc_ref=pc_ref)

  1. The dispersion suppressor is not periodic: it is an open line, and its lattice functions are computed from those at its start. Its start is the end of a periodic arc cell. In SciBmad, the lattice functions at a location are all contained in the normalizing map a there (see Twiss), and a TwissExpr of an open line can start with the a of another TwissExpr. tw_af is the TwissExpr of the forward arc cell cell_af of chapter 1, so:

tw_dsf = TwissExpr(ds_f, a_initial=tw_af.a[end])

TwissExpr of a Beamline with 16 elements (a_initial=a[end])

The dispersion suppressor now always starts with the periodic lattice functions of the arc cell, also when the strengths of qf and qd change.

  1. The targets are the dispersion dx and its derivative dpx (the derivative of px with respect to δ) at the end of the line. Before matching, they are:

value([tw_dsf.dx[end], tw_dsf.dpx[end]])

2-element Vector{Float64}:
 0.051814026590569565
 0.003951153753685826

value computes the values of expressions. To see the dispersion through the line, compute it at every location, tw_dsf.dx[:]:

using CairoMakie
fig = Figure(size=(700, 300))
ax = Axis(fig[1, 1], xlabel="s [m]", ylabel="η [m]")
lines!(ax, value(tw_dsf.s[:]), value(tw_dsf.dx[:]), label="before")
fig

  1. The match varies qff1 and qdf1:

match!(vary=[@knob(qff1.Kn1), @knob(qdf1.Kn1)],
       targets=[tw_dsf.dx[end] == 0, tw_dsf.dpx[end] == 0])

Exercises¶

  1. Reverse dispersion suppressor: Construct the reverse dispersion suppressor, dispsupr, with the quadrupoles qfr1 and qdr1, starting with the periodic lattice functions of the reverse arc cell (tw_ar). Match qfr1 and qdr1 for \(\eta_x = \eta_x' = 0\) at the end. How do the two quadrupole strengths compare to those of the forward suppressor?

  2. Forward and reverse cells: Check that your forward and reverse cells, which both start with focusing quadrupoles, have different periodic beta and alpha functions. Check also that both cells, for the same phase advance of 90°, have exactly the same quadrupole strengths. Explain how this can be the correct solution.

  3. Dispersion derivative: The column dpx is the derivative of the momentum px with respect to δ (Bmad’s etap), and not the derivative of the dispersion with respect to \(s\) (Bmad’s deta_ds). What is the difference between the two? Show that with the lattice being designed they are the same: compare dpx with the slope of dx in a drift, e.g. between the beginning and the end of d1. Hint: the phase space coordinates are defined in Particle Phase Space Coordinates.

  4. Second-order dispersion: The suppressor makes the dispersion \(\partial x/\partial\delta\) zero, but not the second-order dispersion \(\partial^2 x/\partial\delta^2\), the column dx_2. Compute it at the end of the suppressor, as a TwissExpr quantity: value(tw_dsf.dx_2[end]). Is it zero? Why not?

  5. Setting optimization parameters: match! stops after at most 50 iterations by default. Which keyword argument of match! changes this? Hint: look at the documentation with ?match!.

  6. Strength of bends: [Warning! This is a difficult problem!] To a good approximation, a dispersion suppressor from an arc FODO line to a straight FODO line (where the straight FODO cells have the same layout as the arc FODO cells, except that the bends are replaced by drifts) can be constructed using two arc FODO cells, with the first cell having the bend strengths reduced by a factor \(\alpha\) and the second cell with the bend strengths reduced by a factor \(1 - \alpha\). In the case of 90° FODO cells, \(\alpha\) is 0.5. Note: the approximation is only valid in the case of weak bends. Problem: Prove that this is the case, and show that for 60° FODO cells the value of \(\alpha\) is zero.

The solution of exercise 1 is in the lattice file of this chapter, lattices/02-dispersion-suppressor.jl.
