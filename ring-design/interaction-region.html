6. Low-Beta Interaction Region Insertion¶

Introduction¶

In this chapter an interaction region (IR) with a low-beta interaction point (IP) will be constructed, by modifying the quadrupoles of the 6 o’clock straight section as shown in the figures A and B below.

The IR is divided into two parts, which are constructed separately as shown above. IPF is the upstream “forward” section before the IP (labeled IP6), and IPR is the downstream “reverse” section after IP6. At the IP, the beta functions are to be matched to \(\beta_1^* = 0.6\) m and \(\beta_2^* = 0.06\) m and, as is standard for many IPs, the alpha functions \(\alpha_1^*\) and \(\alpha_2^*\) to zero (a “waist”). The surrounding quadrupoles are adjusted to match these IP parameters, while still matching to the periodic solution of the straight section at the ends of the IR furthest from the IP.

Because this region is particularly sensitive, the match is done in two steps:

  1. Match the forward line IPF from the straight section to the IP.

  2. Match the reverse line IPR from the IP, with the lattice functions that IPF actually gives there, to the periodic lattice functions of the straight section.

(In the Bmad version of this tutorial, IPR was matched backwards from the straight section to the IP, and then once more forward to correct the small mismatch at the IP. Because IPR starts with the lattice functions of IPF here, the second match is already exact.)

For IR simulations, SciBmad has crab cavities (CrabCavity) to simulate crabbing with a nonzero crossing angle. A discussion of this is beyond the scope of this tutorial.

Example: Constructing and Matching the IR¶

  1. Start with the ring of chapter 5:

include("lattices/05-ring.jl");

  1. Define the drifts and quadrupoles of the IR, with the dimensions of the figure above. The strengths are to be determined: start with the straight section strengths. Define also the IP itself, as a Marker. Markers have zero length and no effect on the orbit or spin.

@elements begin
  qef1 = Quadrupole(L=0.5, Kn1=k_ss)
  qef2 = Quadrupole(L=0.5, Kn1=-k_ss)
  def1 = Drift(L=20.46)
  qef3 = Quadrupole(L=1.6, Kn1=k_ss)
  def2 = Drift(L=3.76)
  qef4 = Quadrupole(L=1.2, Kn1=-k_ss)
  def3 = Drift(L=5.8)
  ip6 = Marker()
  der3 = Drift(L=5.3)
  qer4 = Quadrupole(L=1.8, Kn1=-k_ss)
  der2 = Drift(L=0.5)
  qer3 = Quadrupole(L=1.4, Kn1=k_ss)
  der1 = Drift(L=23.82)
  qer2 = Quadrupole(L=0.5, Kn1=k_ss)
  qer1 = Quadrupole(L=0.5, Kn1=-k_ss)
end;

  1. Define the upstream and downstream lines. The IP is at the end of ipf. (It is not repeated at the beginning of ipr, so that it is placed once in the ring.)

ipf = [qef1, d1, db, d2, qef2, d1, db, d2, def1, qef3, def2, qef4, def3, ip6]
ipr = [der3, qer4, der2, qer3, der1, qer2, d2, db, d1, qer1, d2, db, d1]
ir_f = Beamline(ipf, species_ref=electron, pc_ref=pc_ref)
ir_r = Beamline(ipr, species_ref=electron, pc_ref=pc_ref)

  1. Match IPF. The beta functions here range from 0.06 m at the IP to several hundred meters in the final focus quadrupoles. A difference of 1 m from the goal means little at 600 m, but a lot at 0.06 m. It is therefore better to match the logarithm of β: then a relative error counts the same everywhere. Targets can be any expression of the quantities of twiss:

match!(vary=[@knob(qef1.Kn1), @knob(qef2.Kn1), @knob(qef3.Kn1), @knob(qef4.Kn1)],
       targets=[log(tw_ipf.beta1[end]) == log(0.6), log(tw_ipf.beta2[end]) == log(0.06),
                tw_ipf.alpha1[end] == 0, tw_ipf.alpha2[end] == 0])

  1. Add the IR into the ring. It replaces the last three straight section cells of sextant 5 and the first three of sextant 7 (each half of the IR is as long as three cells):

sextant5 = [repeat(fodossf, 4); ss_to_arcf; repeat(fodoaf, 20); arc_to_ssf; fodossf; ipf]
sextant7 = [ipr; fodossr; ss_to_arcr; repeat(fodoar, 20); arc_to_ssr; repeat(fodossr, 4)]

ring = Beamline([sextant1; sextant3; sextant5; sextant7; sextant9; sextant11],
                species_ref=electron, pc_ref=pc_ref)
tw = twiss(ring)

Exercises¶

  1. Initial lattice functions for a reflected line: A line can be matched backwards, from its end to its start, by computing the optics of the reflected line reverse(ipr). (This is only correct for elements that are symmetric front to back, such as drifts and quadrupoles.) How must the initial Twiss parameters \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\) be changed for the reflected line? Hint: the Twiss parameters are the same as computed in earlier chapters.

  2. Matching backwards: Match IPR backwards: start the reflected line with the periodic lattice functions of the reverse straight section cell (given with initial, as numbers of a twiss table), and match qer1 to qer4 so that \(\beta_1 = 0.6\) m, \(\beta_2 = 0.06\) m and \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = 0\) at its end, the IP. Start with the strengths found above, each multiplied by 1.01. Do you get the same strengths back? What happens if the initial alpha functions are not changed for the reflected line? (Starting with the straight section strengths, this match stops at a point that is not a solution.)

  3. Logarithms: Start again with the straight section strengths, and match IPR with targets for β instead of log β. What happens? Several matches in this tutorial stop at a point that is not a solution, because small changes of the knobs cannot improve the merit function there. How the targets are written decides which points these are.

  4. No step size: In Bmad, the derivatives for the optimizer are computed with finite differences, with a step size for each variable, and a step size of \(10^{-4}\) instead of \(10^{-6}\) for the quadrupole strengths keeps this match from converging. Why does the step size matter? Why does SciBmad not need one?
